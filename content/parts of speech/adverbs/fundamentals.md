Here you will learn the fundamentals of adverbs in English, such as what they are, how to make them and where to use them. In the pages following, you will learn in detail the different types of adverbs that exist in English along with some examples to get you started.

This guide has been designed with both new and experienced English learners in mind, so even if you've been studying English for a while, there will be something interesting for you to learn.

## What Is An Adverb?
> An adverb is a word that modifies (i.e. describes) a verb, adjective, another adverb or a whole sentence in order to give more detail or context into how, when, where, how often, or how much an action is being performed.

In English, many (but not all) adverbs end in "-ly", such as slow**ly**, happi**ly**, quiet**ly**, etc. However, there are some adverbs that don't follow this rule. For example, you can run fast, but not fast*ly*.

Similarly, some English adverbs are identical to their adjective forms (e.g. late → late) while others change completely (e.g. good → well). Adverbs can never modify nouns.

Word order with adverbs is flexible depending on the type of adverb and context they are used in. Some adverbs come before the verb they modify, some come at the end of a sentence and others sit at the beginning. See <!-- see note here -->.

Adverbs are useful for conveying **how**, **when**, **where**, **how often** and **how much** an action occurs or is performed, and as such are essential grammar if you want to communicate fluently in English.

## Types Of Adverbs
There are 8 main categories of adverbs in English.

- [[time|Adverbs of Time]]
Used to provide information on when something has happened, is happening or will happen.
Adverbs of Time can express an absolute time (e.g., tomorrow) or a relative time (e.g., soon).
<br>

- [[frequency|Adverbs of Frequency]]
Used to specify how often an action occurs or is carried out.
Adverbs of Frequency can express a relative rate (e.g. always, never) or a specific interval (e.g. every two minutes).
<br>

- [[place|Adverbs of Place]]
Used to describe where something occurs or exists.
Adverbs of Place can express a direction (e.g. north) or a spatial relationship (e.g. under, nearby).
<br>

- [[manner|Adverbs of Manner]]
Used to provide information on *how* someone does something.
Adverbs of Manner are most often used with action verbs.
<br>

- [[degree|Adverbs of Degree]]
Used to provide information concerning intensity or how much something is performed.
Adverbs of Degree can strengthen or weaken the word they describe (e.g. extremely, slightly, completely).
<br>

- [[certainty|Adverbs of Certainty]]
Used to provide information on how sure or confident a speaker is about an action or event.
Adverbs of Certainty express levels of probability (e.g. definitely, probably, possibly, certainly).
<br>

- [[conjunctive|Conjunctive Adverbs]]
Used to connect two independent clauses or sentences to show the logical relationship between them.
Conjunctive Adverbs can transition between ideas by showing contrast, cause and effect, addition or sequence (e.g. however, therefore, moreover, meanwhile).
<br>

- [[interrogative|Interrogative Adverbs]]
Used to introduce a direct or indirect question regarding time, location, reason or manner.
Interrogative Adverbs ask for details that would typically be answered with an adverbial phrase (e.g. when, where, why, how).

## Creating Adverbs

There are two main groups of adverbs in English: those formed systematically from adjectives, and those that are flat or irregular standalone words.

Adverbs in the second group — such as irregular adverbs or adverbs formed from prepositional phrases — require memorable exposure as they do not follow standard suffix patterns.

However, adverbs in the first group — those formed from adjectives — follow straightforward spelling rules that make them easy to create and understand once you know the base adjective.

## Using Adverbs

### To Modify Verbs

To have an adverb modify a verb, place the adverb either after the verb/object, or directly before the main verb (depending on the adverb type).

For example:

> 《１》 🇬🇧   Let's study for the exam **together**.
>　　　 🇫🇷   試験を**一緒に**勉強しましょう。

> 《２》 🇬🇧   I am walking **slowly**.
>　　　 🇫🇷   **ゆっくり**歩いています。

> 《３》 🇬🇧   She **quickly** finished her homework.
>　　　 🇫🇷   彼女は**すばやく**宿題を終えた。

### To Modify Adjectives

To have an adverb modify an adjective, place the adverb directly **before** the adjective you wish to modify.

For example:

> 《１》 🇬🇧   The vaccine was **hardly** painful.
>　　　 🇫🇷   ワクチンは**ほとんど**痛くなかった。

> 《２》 🇬🇧   That is **probably** impossible.
>　　　 🇫🇷   それは**たぶん**無理です。

> 《３》 🇬🇧   This room is **extremely** cold.
>　　　 🇫🇷   この部屋は**非常**に寒い。

### To Modify Adverbs

It is possible to use an adverb to modify another adverb. Place the modifying adverb directly **before** the adverb it describes.

For example:

> 《１》 🇬🇧   The teacher speaks **very kindly**.
>　　　 🇫🇷   先生は**本当に優しく**話します。

> 《２》 🇬🇧   He runs **remarkably fast**.
>　　　 🇫🇷   彼は**驚くほど速く**走る。

### To Modify Entire Sentences

Some adverbs (known as sentence adverbs or disjuncts) can be placed at the very start of a sentence, followed by a comma, to modify the entire statement.

For example:

> 《１》 🇬🇧   **Fortunately**, we caught the last train.
>　　　 🇫🇷   **幸いなことに**、終電に間に合った。

> 《２》 🇬🇧   **Honestly**, I don't know the answer.
>　　　 🇫🇷   **正直に言うと**、答えが分からない。

### As Standalone Words / Conversational Responses

In spoken English, many adverbs can stand alone as complete single-word responses or exclamations.

> 《１》 🇬🇧   **Finally!**
> Wrapper text: **やっと！**

> 《２》 🇬🇧   **Definitely!**
> Wrapper text: **絶対に！ / もちろん！**

> 《３》 🇬🇧   **Honestly?**
> Wrapper text: **本当に？ / 正直なところ？**

### 1. Structural Rules & Mechanics

* **Adverb Placement Rules (Front, Mid, End Position):**
Unlike Japanese (where adverbs almost always precede the word they modify), English adverbs change position depending on their type:
* **Front position** (Sentence adverbs/Time): *"**Yesterday**, I went to the shop."*
* **Mid position** (Frequency/Degree — between subject and verb, or after auxiliary verbs): *"I have **already** eaten"* / *"She **always** wakes up early."*
* **End position** (Manner/Place): *"He drives **carefully**"* / *"She lives **there**."*


* **The "Verb + Object + Adverb" Rule (Golden Rule):**
Never place an adverb between a verb and its direct object.
* ❌ *I speak fluently English.*
* ✅ *I speak English fluently.* / *I fluently speak English.*


* **Order of Multiple Adverbs (M-P-T Rule):**
When using multiple adverbs at the end of a sentence, they follow the **Manner → Place → Time** sequence:
* *Example:* "She sang **beautifully** *(Manner)* **at the concert** *(Place)* **last night** *(Time)*."

### 2. High-Frequency Pitfalls & Confusions

* **False "-ly" Words (Adjectives ending in -ly):**
Learners often mistake words like *friendly*, *lovely*, *lonely*, *silly*, and *ugly* for adverbs because of their suffix.
* *Correction:* To use them adverbially, learners must use a phrase: *"She spoke to me in a **friendly manner**"* (not *"She spoke to me friendlily"*).


* **Meanings That Change with "-ly":**
Adding *-ly* to certain words changes their definition entirely rather than creating a manner form:
* **Hard** (with effort) vs. **Hardly** (almost not at all)
* **Late** (after the expected time) vs. **Lately** (recently)
* **Near** (close by) vs. **Nearly** (almost)
* **Free** (without cost) vs. **Freely** (without constraint)
* **High** (elevation) vs. **Highly** (extremely / with high regard)


* **Adverb vs. Adjective (Good vs. Well):**
One of the most frequent errors in ESL writing:
* *Good* is an adjective (*"She is a good writer"*).
* *Well* is the corresponding adverb (*"She writes well"*).

### 3. Advanced & Specialty Functions

* **Conjunctive Adverbs (Discourse Connectors):**
Adverbs used to transition between ideas or clauses (e.g., *however*, *therefore*, *moreover*, *furthermore*, *otherwise*). Including punctuation rules (semi-colon before, comma after: `; however,`) adds significant utility to the guide.
* **Comparative & Superlative Forms of Adverbs:**
* Regular (-ly): *more carefully* / *most carefully*
* Short/Flat adverbs: *faster* / *fastest*, *harder* / *hardest*
* Irregular: *better* / *best* (from *well*), *worse* / *worst* (from *badly*)


* **Adverbs modifying Prepositional Phrases & Clauses:**
